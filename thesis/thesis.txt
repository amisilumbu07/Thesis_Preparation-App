   长期时间序列预测（LTSF）是电力系统运行的基础，直接关系到发电调度、需求响
应与设备维护等关键决策。近年来，以Transformer为代表的自注意力模型在多项公开
预测基准上取得了领先性能，但其在有限硬件条件下的实际表现，以及在偏离标准基
准数据（如工业现场数据）时的稳定性，尚未得到充分研究。本文以此为切入点，在单
块6GB显存的消费级GPU上，针对气象（WTH）、电力变压器温度（ETTh1）、钢铁工业
能耗（STEEL）与伦敦智能电表（Smart    Meters）四个数据集，对三种Transformer预测模
型（Informer、Autoformer、PatchTST）与四种循环神经网络基线模型（LSTM、BiLSTM、
GRU及一种精简单层LSTM配置）进行了系统的对比实验。
   研究结果表明，PatchTST在特征维度更高、结构更丰富的数据集（如ETTh1与智能
电表数据）上具有明显的准确率优势，而Informer则在气象数据上表现更优，并在所有
数据集上均保持最快的训练速度，说明Transformer模型间的优劣并非绝对，而是依赖
于具体数据特性。更为重要的是，在钢铁工业数据集上，Informer与Autoformer在训练
首个周期即出现数值发散（损失值变为NaN），且这一现象与模型参数量无关；而全部
四种循环神经网络模型及PatchTST均能稳定训练。本文进一步分析发现，其根本原因
并非通道混合本身，而在于Informer与Autoformer在跨通道计算注意力或自相关系数
时未对数值进行有界处理，而循环网络的门控机制（Sigmoid、Tanh）及PatchTST的通道
独立注意力机制则天然具备数值有界性，从而避免了训练失稳。基于以上发现，本文
提出了一套兼顾硬件条件与数据特性的模型选择框架，为实际部署中的负荷预测模型
选型提供了具有可操作性的依据。

                                    ABSTRACT
    Long-term time-series forecasting (LTSF) underpins critical decisions in power system
operation, including generation scheduling, demand response, and equipment maintenance.
While Transformer-based architectures have recently achieved leading performance on
public forecasting benchmarks, their practical behaviour under constrained hardware, and
their stability on data that departs from curated benchmarks — such as industrial process
data — remain insufficiently studied. This thesis addresses this gap through a systematic,
hardware-constrained comparison of three Transformer-based forecasters (Informer,
Autoformer, PatchTST) and four recurrent baselines (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and a
minimal single-layer LSTM configuration), evaluated on a single 6GB-VRAM consumer
GPU across four datasets: Weather (WTH), Electricity Transformer Temperature (ETTh1),
Steel Industry Energy Consumption (STEEL), and Smart Meters in London.
    The results show that PatchTST achieves a clear accuracy advantage on datasets with
richer, higher-dimensional feature structure (ETTh1 and Smart Meters), while Informer
performs best on the Weather dataset and is consistently the fastest architecture to train
across every dataset — demonstrating that no single Transformer variant is unconditionally
superior, and that architectural ranking is data-dependent. More significantly, on the Steel
Industry dataset, Informer and Autoformer both diverge numerically (NaN losses) within
the first training epoch regardless of model parameter count, while all four recurrent
baselines and PatchTST train stably. Further analysis attributes this not to channel-mixing
itself, but to the fact that Informer and Autoformer compute unbounded attention or
auto-correlation scores across channels, whereas the gating mechanisms (sigmoid, tanh) of
the recurrent networks and the channel-independent attention of PatchTST are inherently
bounded, avoiding the numerical instability observed. Based on these findings, this thesis
proposes a hardware- and data-aware model-selection framework that offers practical,
actionable guidance for choosing forecasting architectures in real-world deployment.

1.1​Background and Motivation

     The remarkable success of Transformer architectures in Natural Language Processing
(NLP) and Computer Vision (CV) has motivated substantial interest in extending them to
time-series data, where the self-attention mechanism's capacity to model long-range
dependencies and complex temporal interactions offers a compelling alternative to
conventional sequence models [1].
     This interest is reinforced by a global energy landscape undergoing rapid
transformation. Global electricity demand is projected to grow substantially in the coming
decades as digitalization and electrification accelerate [2], intensifying the need for
forecasting tools that support grid stability, efficient resource allocation, and the integration
of variable renewable generation into modern power systems [3]. Precise long-term load
forecasting has consequently become central not only to operational efficiency but to the
broader energy transition.
     Time-series forecasting has historically relied on statistical methods such as ARIMA
together with Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM)
networks to predict future values from sequential historical data [4, 5]. LSTM-based models
in particular remain widely deployed for load forecasting owing to their comparatively low
computational cost and mature tooling [6]. However, Long-Term Time Series Forecasting
(LTSF) requires models to map extended historical windows onto extended future horizons,
a setting in which recurrent architectures are prone to vanishing or exploding gradients and
consequently struggle to retain information across very long sequences.
     The Transformer architecture addresses long-range dependency modelling through
self-attention rather than recurrence [1]; however, the standard self-attention mechanism
incurs quadratic time and memory complexity with respect to input sequence length, and
the conventional encoder–decoder structure is prone to information bottlenecks when
generating long-horizon forecasts. These limitations motivated a succession of
                                               10
LTSF-specific Transformer variants: the Informer introduces a ProbSparse self-attention
mechanism to reduce this complexity [7]; the Autoformer replaces point-wise attention with
a decomposition architecture and auto-correlation mechanism to more effectively isolate
trend and seasonal components [8]; and, more recently, PatchTST segments each series into
sub-series patches and processes each variable independently, substantially reducing the
effective attention cost while improving robustness to distribution shift [9].
     Despite these architectural advances, a growing body of subsequent work has
questioned whether the reported superiority of Transformer-based LTSF models is
unconditional. Zeng et al. demonstrated that a considerably simpler linear model could
match or exceed several Transformer variants on standard benchmarks [10], and related
work has shown that processing each variable independently — a central design choice in
PatchTST — reflects a genuine trade-off between representational capacity and robustness
rather than a strict improvement [11]. Moreover, the datasets on which these comparisons
are typically conducted — meteorological records, transformer temperature series, and
similar benchmarks — exhibit continuity and moderate scale ranges that may not be
representative of real industrial energy consumption data, and such comparisons are rarely
conducted under the memory and compute constraints faced by non-server-class
deployment. This gap between idealized benchmark evaluation and practical deployment
realities constitutes the central motivation of this research.

     Accurate    long-term     load   forecasting    has   significant   academic,   economic,
environmental, and industrial importance. It is an essential component of smart-grid
operations because it supports efficient resource allocation, effective demand-response
management, and the stable integration of renewable energy generation . By accurately
predicting future electricity demand, grid operators can improve system planning, reduce
operational uncertainty, and maintain a reliable balance between energy supply and
consumption.
     For industrial organizations and utility providers, forecasting models must not only
achieve high predictive accuracy but also operate within the computational resources
available in real-world deployment environments. In practice, these resources are often

 computational efficiency and practical usability.[9][11]

 and regular benchmark datasets commonly used in existing studies, industrial energy data

1.2​Problem Statement

     Although Transformer-based models hold considerable promise for long-term
time-series forecasting, several critical challenges limit the direct transferability of their
reported benchmark performance to practical load-forecasting deployments. First, the
standard self-attention mechanism is not computationally efficient and imposes substantial
memory overhead due to its quadratic complexity with respect to input sequence length [1],
a constraint that becomes acute under the limited hardware budgets available to many
utilities and research groups. Second, although efficient variants such as Informer,
Autoformer, and PatchTST have reported strong results on their original evaluation
benchmarks [7, 8, 9], their robustness and generalization to energy data exhibiting the
extreme scale heterogeneity and discontinuous behaviour characteristic of industrial
consumption remain largely unexamined. Third, existing comparative studies rarely report
forecasting accuracy jointly with the computational cost — training time, memory
footprint, and parameter count — required to achieve it, making the trade-off between the
two difficult to assess for practitioners operating under hardware constraints. Finally, where
Transformer-based models fail outright rather than merely underperforming, the literature
offers limited diagnostic detail regarding the data conditions responsible, restricting the
ability of practitioners to anticipate or mitigate such failures in deployment. This study
addresses these gaps by conducting a systematic, jointly reported evaluation of
Transformer-based and recurrent forecasting architectures across datasets of varying

                                             13
statistical regularity, under a constrained and explicitly documented hardware budget, and
extends the assessment to a failure case on industrial data that is largely absent from the
existing literature.

1.3​Key Issues and Research Questions

     Long-term time-series forecasting has become an essential task in energy management
and industrial operations, as it supports applications such as demand planning, resource
allocation, and predictive decision-making. Although Transformer-based models have
achieved promising results in sequence forecasting, several intrinsic challenges remain,
particularly when they are deployed under practical computational constraints. These
challenges include the high computational cost of processing long sequences, limited
robustness to heterogeneous industrial data, and the lack of a comprehensive understanding
of the conditions under which different forecasting architectures succeed or fail. To address
these challenges, the proposed study aims to explore and answer the following research
questions:

RQ1. Under a fixed, constrained hardware budget, how do Transformer-based models
      (Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST) compare to recurrent baselines (LSTM, BiLSTM,
      GRU) in forecasting accuracy and computational efficiency for long-term load
      forecasting?

RQ2. To what extent do Transformer-based models demonstrate robustness and generalization
      when applied to industrial energy consumption data exhibiting extreme scale
      heterogeneity and discontinuous behaviour, as opposed to standard meteorological or
      residential benchmarks?
RQ3. Where Transformer-based models fail to train or produce degenerate predictions, what
      data characteristics are associated with these failures, and what mechanisms plausibly
      account for them?
RQ4. Can the findings from these comparisons be synthesized into practical, hardware- and
      data-aware guidance for selecting a forecasting architecture under a given combination
      of data characteristics and computational constraints?


     To address these research questions, the study evaluates several efficient Transformer
architectures designed to mitigate the computational challenges associated with

BiLSTM, GRU, and a minimal single-layer LSTM configuration. Their forecasting

     Furthermore,    the   study   employs     appropriate   datasets, forecasting      metrics,

hardware limitations.
     In conclusion, the contributions of this study include addressing the computational

Transformer models on industrial energy-consumption data, and providing a rigorous
comparison with established recurrent baselines. The study also seeks to identify the data
properties associated with model failure and to formulate practical recommendations for
selecting forecasting architectures according to available computational resources and
dataset characteristics. Limitations and future directions are also discussed, highlighting
opportunities for further research into efficient, robust, and deployment-oriented time-series
forecasting.

1.5​Scope and limitations

        This research concentrates on long-term energy load forecasting, comparing three
Transformer-based models (Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST) against four recurrent
baselines (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and a minimal single-layer LSTM configuration) across
four datasets spanning a range of statistical regularity — from continuous meteorological
and residential consumption data to discontinuous industrial energy consumption data. All
experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 2060 GPU with 6GB of VRAM, a
configuration selected to represent constrained, non-server-class hardware rather than to
establish the maximum achievable performance of any given architecture.
         While the principles and findings of this research may be transferable to other
time-series forecasting domains, such as traffic flow or financial markets, this study
exclusively addresses energy consumption data as its use case, and the selection of specific
models and baselines defines the boundary of the comparison; generalization to other
Transformer variants or baseline models is beyond its immediate scope. Hyperparameters
were held fixed across comparable model categories to preserve comparability rather than
individually tuned to optimality, which may understate the best-case performance
achievable by any single architecture. Where a failure mode is identified and an explanatory
mechanism proposed, this thesis treats that mechanism as a hypothesis supported by the
available evidence rather than a definitively established causal finding, pending
confirmatory ablation experiments identified as future work. Finally, reliance on publicly
available datasets means the study is constrained by their characteristics, quality, and
                                             17
documentation.

2.1​Overview


     Time-series forecasting has long served as a cornerstone of decision-making in
domains ranging from energy systems and financial markets to climate science and
industrial operations. With the accelerating digitization of infrastructure and the
proliferation of high-resolution sensor data, the demand for accurate, scalable, and robust
forecasting models has intensified, particularly as global electricity demand continues to
grow and power systems incorporate increasing shares of variable renewable generation [2].
Traditional statistical approaches, while interpretable and theoretically well-grounded, often
falter when confronted with the nonlinearity, high dimensionality, and long-range temporal
dependencies characteristic of modern real-world datasets, including the electrical load data
central to this thesis [3].
     The advent of deep learning has ushered in a new era of forecasting methodology, in
which neural architectures — recurrent networks, convolutional networks, and, more
recently, Transformer-based models — have demonstrated substantial advances in
predictive performance. Recurrent neural networks and their gated variants (LSTM, GRU)
addressed the linearity constraints of classical statistical models by learning nonlinear
temporal dependencies directly from data [5, 6], while convolutional and hybrid
architectures introduced parallelizable alternatives capable of efficiently extracting local
temporal patterns. Most recently, Transformer architectures, originally developed for
natural language processing, have rapidly permeated the time-series community owing to
their capacity to model long-range interactions in parallel, without the sequential
bottlenecks inherent to recurrent computation [1]. This shift has catalyzed the emergence of
specialized Transformer variants tailored explicitly for Long-Term Time-Series Forecasting
(LTSF), including Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, and PatchTST, each introducing
architectural refinements intended to address the vanilla Transformer's quadratic
                                             19
computational      complexity,      memory       inefficiency,    and     misalignment   with
time-series-specific structures such as trend, seasonality, and sparsity [7, 8, 9].
     The literature review chapter discusses various types of time series predictions
methods, as illustrated in the following diagram:

2.2​Traditional Methods of Time Series Prediction

     Traditional time-series forecasting methods are rooted in statistical modelling
principles that rely on assumptions of stationarity, linearity, and well-defined stochastic
processes [4]. These approaches have historically underpinned applications in energy
systems, finance, and climate forecasting, offering interpretable models that provide clear
insight into temporal dynamics, and they remain useful for establishing baseline
performance on stable datasets. However, their linear assumptions, limited capacity to
represent   multivariate dependencies, and difficulty with long-horizon prediction
increasingly restrict their applicability in modern, high-frequency, high-dimensional
settings such as smart grids and industrial energy monitoring — limitations that have driven
the field toward the nonlinear, data-driven approaches reviewed in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

2.2.1​Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average(ARIMA)

     The Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, formalized by Box
and Jenkins, remains one of the most widely used classical forecasting methods [14]. It
integrates three components: autoregression (AR), which models the relationship between
an observation and its own past values; differencing (I), applied to achieve stationarity in
non-stationary series; and moving averages (MA), which account for correlation among
past forecast errors. ARIMA is particularly effective for univariate series exhibiting stable
statistical properties, and its interpretable structure makes it a preferred choice where model
transparency is valued. However, its reliance on linear assumptions limits performance in
the presence of nonlinear dynamics or high-frequency fluctuations; Hippert et al.
demonstrated that while ARIMA could reliably forecast short-term electricity load, its
accuracy deteriorated markedly under volatile consumption patterns or high-dimensional
input features, motivating the need for more flexible modelling approaches of the kind
reviewed later in this chapter [15].


.




                                              22
2.2.2​Seasonal ARIMA and Related Seasonal Methods

     Building upon ARIMA, Seasonal ARIMA (SARIMA) incorporates explicit seasonal
components to account for periodic variation, introducing additional parameters to model
recurring patterns over defined seasonal intervals, making it well suited to datasets with
strong cyclical behaviour such as daily or weekly electricity consumption. Taylor's work on
triple seasonal methods for short-term electricity demand forecasting demonstrated that
explicitly modelling multiple superimposed seasonal cycles — daily, weekly, and annual —
could yield meaningful accuracy improvements over standard seasonal decomposition on
utility load data, while also showing that performance degraded during periods of abrupt
demand shifts not captured by the fitted seasonal structure [16]. This sensitivity to structural
change is a recurring theme that motivates the nonlinear approaches discussed
subsequently.

2.2.3​Vector Autoregression(VAR)

     The Vector Autoregression (VAR) model extends the autoregressive framework to
multivariate settings, modelling each variable as a linear combination of its own past values
and those of other variables in the system. This property makes VAR useful where multiple
interdependent series influence one another, such as electricity load and ambient
temperature. Lütkepohl's comprehensive treatment of multiple time-series analysis
illustrates VAR's utility in economic and energy contexts while emphasizing its constraints:
the “curse of dimensionality” reduces scalability as the number of variables grows, and the
model's linear interaction assumption limits its effectiveness on the nonlinear relationships
present in many modern datasets, including the multivariate industrial energy data
considered in this thesis [17].

2.2.4​Other Classical Methods (VAR/ECM/ETS/Exponential Smoothing)

     Beyond ARIMA, SARIMA, and VAR, several additional statistical approaches have
been widely used in traditional forecasting. Error Correction Models capture long-term
equilibrium relationships among cointegrated variables, following the foundational

                                              23
cointegration framework of Engle and Granger, and are valuable in multivariate systems
where series move together over time [19]. Exponential Smoothing State Space Models
(ETS), formalized within a unified state-space framework by Hyndman et al., rely on
weighted averages of past observations and can incorporate trend and seasonal components,
allowing them to adapt to gradual change while remaining computationally efficient for
short-term forecasting in stable environments [18]. Collectively, these methods provide a
suite of tools for modelling linear or quasi-linear temporal dynamics, offering
complementary capabilities depending on dataset characteristics, even as contemporary
forecasting has moved toward the data-driven, nonlinear approaches reviewed next.

2.3​Neural Network-Based Time Series Predictions

    The limitations of traditional statistical methods — particularly their linearity
assumptions and difficulty representing long-range dependencies — motivated the
development of neural network-based forecasting approaches capable of modelling
complex nonlinear relationships and multivariate dependencies across diverse domains. The
evolution of neural forecasting methods can be broadly categorized into recurrent
architectures and convolutional or hybrid architectures, each addressing specific challenges
in temporal modelling.

2.3.1​Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)

    Recurrent Neural Networks were among the first neural architectures designed
specifically for sequential data. By maintaining internal memory states that encode
information from previous time steps, RNNs capture temporal dependencies crucial to
accurate forecasting; however, early RNN variants suffered from vanishing and exploding
gradient problems that limited their ability to learn long-range dependencies. Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks were introduced specifically to overcome this
limitation through a gated memory-cell design that regulates the flow of information
through the network, allowing relevant information to be retained over substantially longer
sequences than a standard RNN [5]. Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) later offered a
simplified gating variant with fewer parameters, achieving comparable performance at
reduced computational cost [22]. LSTM-based models in particular have been extensively
                                            24
applied to load forecasting; Kong et al. demonstrated that an LSTM recurrent architecture
could reliably capture the nonlinear temporal dynamics of residential electricity
consumption, establishing LSTM as a strong and computationally efficient baseline for
load-forecasting research that this thesis adopts accordingly [6].
     Extensions such as Bidirectional LSTMs (BiLSTMs) allow a model to access both past
and future context during training by processing the sequence in both temporal directions,
improving predictive accuracy for sequences with dependencies that are not purely causal
[21]. Attention-augmented recurrent architectures further enhance model capability by
allowing the network to weight the most relevant segments of historical input dynamically;
Sehovac and Grolinger showed that sequence-to-sequence LSTM architectures equipped
with attention significantly outperformed standard recurrent baselines in electricity load
forecasting, underscoring the value of dynamic temporal weighting even within a
fundamentally recurrent architecture [20]. Despite these refinements, RNN-family models
face an inherent limitation: their sequential processing impedes parallelization, training is
comparatively slow, and very long-range dependencies remain difficult to capture in
high-frequency or irregular datasets — the central motivation for the attention-based
architectures reviewed in Section 2.4..

2.3.2​Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Hybrid Architectures

     Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), originally developed for spatial data such as
images, have been successfully adapted to time-series forecasting through one-dimensional
convolutions applied along the temporal dimension. Bai et al. showed that Temporal
Convolutional Networks (TCNs), which use dilated causal convolutions to expand the
receptive field, could match or exceed recurrent architectures across a range of
sequence-modelling tasks while offering more stable gradients and fully parallelizable
computation, making CNN-based models considerably more efficient than sequential RNNs
for long input sequences [23]. Hybrid architectures such as LSTNet, introduced by Lai et
al., combine convolutional layers for extracting local, short-term patterns with recurrent
layers for modelling longer-term dependencies, and were shown to be particularly effective
on multivariate forecasting tasks by leveraging convolution for feature extraction and
recurrence for sequential memory [24]. Empirical evidence from this line of work suggests
                                              25
that convolutional architectures excel at capturing repeating seasonal and cyclical structure
and are less prone to gradient instability than plain RNNs, though their effectiveness can
decline on irregularly sampled data or extremely long sequences without hybridization — a
limitation that motivated the shift toward fully attention-based modelling described next.


2.4​Transformers-Based Time Series Prediction

     While RNN and CNN-based architectures advanced the capability of neural
forecasting, both face challenges in capturing very long-range dependencies or processing
extremely long sequences efficiently. Transformer-based models, originally developed for
natural language processing, replace sequential recurrence with self-attention mechanisms,
enabling parallel processing and direct modelling of interactions across all time steps [1].
However, adapting Transformers to time-series forecasting revealed challenges specific to
the domain, including quadratic computational complexity, inefficient long-horizon
prediction, and a lack of alignment with time-series-specific structure such as seasonality,
trend, and sparsity. The following subsections review the foundational architecture and the
principal LTSF-specific variants developed to address these challenges.



2.4.1​Foundations of the Transformer architecture

     A foundational understanding of the Transformer architecture is essential to situating
the LTSF-specific variants reviewed subsequently. The vanilla Transformer, introduced by
Vaswani et al., follows an encoder–decoder structure in which both encoder and decoder are
composed of multiple identical blocks; each encoder block consists of a multi-head
self-attention module and a position-wise feed-forward network, while each decoder block
additionally inserts a cross-attention module between the self-attention and feed-forward
components [1].
     Unlike LSTM or RNN architectures, the vanilla Transformer contains no recurrence
and instead relies on positional encoding, added to the input embeddings, to represent
sequence order. Absolute positional encodings assign each position a fixed or learned
embedding vector, with learned variants offering greater flexibility, as demonstrated in the
BERT architecture of Devlin et al. [25]. Relative positional encoding schemes instead
                                             26
model the pairwise relationship between input positions directly; Shaw et al. proposed
adding a learnable relative positional embedding to the attention keys, motivated by the
intuition that relative position is often more informative than absolute position for sequence
modelling [27]. Convolutional alternatives to positional encoding, such as the convolutional
sequence-to-sequence architecture of Gehring et al., demonstrated that positional
information could also be embedded implicitly through convolutional structure rather than
explicit encoding [26].
     The core computational primitive of the Transformer is scaled dot-product attention
over a Query-Key-Value (QKV) representation, in which queries and keys are compared to
produce attention weights that are applied to the values; multi-head attention extends this
by computing several such attention functions in parallel over learned projections of the
input, allowing the model to attend to different representation subspaces simultaneously [1].
Each attention sub-layer is followed by a position-wise feed-forward network, and residual
connections combined with layer normalization are applied around each sub-layer to
stabilize training in deep stacks of these blocks. It is this attention mechanism, and
specifically its quadratic cost with respect to sequence length, that motivated the
efficiency-oriented variants reviewed below.

2.4.2​Informer

     Informer introduces ProbSparse self-attention, which computes only the most
informative query-key pairs rather than the full pairwise attention matrix, reducing attention
complexity from O(L²) to O(L log L) with respect to sequence length L [7]. The model
further incorporates self-attention distilling, which progressively down-samples the
sequence representation within the encoder to reduce memory usage across stacked layers,
and a generative-style decoder capable of predicting the entire output sequence in a single
forward pass rather than through iterative, step-by-step decoding. Zhou et al. demonstrated
Informer's superiority over the standard Transformer on electricity load and traffic
forecasting benchmarks, particularly for sequences exceeding several hundred time steps,
establishing it as one of the first LTSF-specific Transformer variants to gain wide adoption
in the load-forecasting literature [7].


                                               27
2.4.3​Autoformer


     Autoformer addresses long-horizon forecasting by explicitly decomposing the input
sequence into trend-cyclical and seasonal components via moving-average-based
decomposition blocks embedded directly within the model architecture. Its auto-correlation
mechanism replaces standard point-wise attention with a periodicity-based weighting
scheme that identifies and aggregates sub-series at the most relevant periodic lags, allowing
the model to capture repeating temporal patterns more efficiently than dense self-attention
while simultaneously reducing computational cost [8]. Wu et al. reported that Autoformer
achieved strong performance on energy and traffic datasets exhibiting pronounced cyclical
behaviour, outperforming both the standard Transformer and Informer on several
long-horizon benchmarks, though — as the experimental results in Chapter 4 of this thesis
show — its slower initial convergence relative to Informer on some datasets suggests that
benchmark-level superiority does not always translate into faster or more stable training
under constrained compute budgets [8].

2.4.4​FEDformer

     FEDformer operates in the frequency domain, applying the Fast Fourier Transform to
identify a compact set of dominant spectral components rather than processing the sequence
entirely in the time domain. Zhou et al. showed that this frequency-domain formulation,
combined      with      a   mixture-of-experts    decomposition      mechanism,      reduces
sequence-modelling complexity toward linear time while improving robustness to noise,
allowing FEDformer to effectively capture periodicity in energy, traffic, and industrial
datasets, particularly where high-frequency fluctuations are present [28]. FEDformer is
included in this literature review for architectural completeness, though it was not among
the models empirically evaluated in this thesis; its frequency-domain approach nonetheless
represents   an      important   complementary    direction   to   the    patch-based    and
decomposition-based methods that are evaluated.




                                             28
2.4.5​PatchTST

     PatchTST introduces a patching strategy that segments each input series into shorter
sub-sequences, or patches, each of which is treated as a single token within the attention
mechanism, reducing the effective sequence length from L to N ≪ L patches and
correspondingly reducing attention complexity toward O(N²) [9]. Combined with
channel-independent processing — in which each variate of a multivariate series is treated
as an independent univariate input stream rather than being fused with other variates at the
input stage — PatchTST achieves improved accuracy, faster training, and lower memory
consumption relative to earlier Transformer variants. Nie et al. demonstrated that PatchTST
consistently outperformed prior Transformer models and recurrent baselines on
high-dimensional, high-variance benchmark datasets including Weather and Electricity
Transformer Temperature (ETTh1), particularly under limited GPU memory budgets, a
finding directly consistent with the accuracy and efficiency results obtained for PatchTST
in this thesis [9].

2.4.6​Channel Independence and Sub-Series Patching: Recent Advances

     The channel-independence design choice introduced by PatchTST has, since 2023,
become an active research axis in its own right, with subsequent work examining both its
benefits and its costs. Zeng et al. challenged the premise that architectural complexity
translates into forecasting accuracy, showing that a considerably simpler linear model could
match or exceed several Transformer variants, including early patch-based models, on
standard LTSF benchmarks — a result that directly motivates this thesis's emphasis on
jointly reporting accuracy and computational cost rather than accuracy alone [10]. In direct
response to the channel-independence question, Han et al. formalized the underlying
trade-off explicitly, demonstrating that channel-independent training sacrifices cross-variate
representational capacity in exchange for improved robustness to distribution shift, a
finding with direct relevance to the heterogeneous, multivariate industrial data examined in
Chapter 4 of this thesis [11].
     Other recent work has pushed in the opposite direction, arguing that discarding
cross-variate information entirely is suboptimal for datasets with strong inter-variable

                                             29
structure. Zhang and Yan's Crossformer explicitly models cross-dimension dependency
alongside temporal dependency through a two-stage attention mechanism [29], while Liu et
al.'s iTransformer inverts the conventional Transformer input representation, treating each
variate as a token and each time step as a feature, to allow the model to attend across
variates directly [30]. Wang et al.'s CARD architecture proposes channel-aligned attention
specifically to recover useful inter-variable structure that channel-independent processing
discards [31], and Ilbert et al. attribute part of the accuracy gap between simple linear
models and Transformers to the sharper loss landscapes induced by channel-independent
training, proposing sharpness-aware minimization as a mitigation [32]. Chen et al.'s
Pathformer further generalizes the patching concept introduced by PatchTST into a
multi-scale, adaptive patching scheme capable of selecting patch granularity dynamically
based on the input series [33]. Reversible Instance Normalization (RevIN), proposed by
Kim et al., addresses a related but distinct problem — per-window distribution shift — by
normalizing each input window prior to processing and reversing the transformation on the
output, and is now used within PatchTST and several subsequent architectures specifically
to improve robustness to non-stationary input statistics [13]. Taken together, this body of
work indicates that channel independence, sub-series patching, and instance normalization
are not settled design choices but active trade-offs whose appropriateness depends on the
statistical structure of the target data — precisely the dependency this thesis investigates
empirically using both regular benchmark data and industrial energy data with markedly
different statistical properties. Recent surveys by Wen et al. and Su et al. provide
comprehensive taxonomies of this rapidly expanding design space but explicitly note the
continued scarcity of controlled efficiency comparisons and documented failure cases in the
published literature, a gap this thesis directly addresses [34, 35].

2.5​Summary and research gap

     The evolution of time-series forecasting has progressed from linear statistical models
(Sections 2.2.1–2.2.4) to nonlinear neural architectures — recurrent and convolutional
(Section 2.3) — and, most recently, to purpose-built Transformer architectures (Section
2.4). Each paradigm shift has addressed specific limitations of its predecessor: neural
architectures overcame the linearity constraints of ARIMA-family models; convolutional
and hybrid architectures improved short-term pattern extraction and parallel computational
efficiency relative to recurrence; Transformers overcame the sequential processing
bottleneck of RNNs through self-attention; and specialized variants such as Informer,
Autoformer, and PatchTST refined the Transformer specifically for the demands of
long-term forecasting. Most recently, the channel-independence and patching literature
reviewed in Section 2.4.6 has begun to interrogate whether these architectural refinements
represent unconditional improvements or context-dependent trade-offs.
     Despite this progress, four research gaps remain evident from the literature reviewed
in this chapter:
●​      Hardware-Aware Model Selection: Real-world deployment, particularly on
        constrained hardware with limited GPU memory, requires explicit joint
        consideration of architectural accuracy and computational efficiency, yet the
        published comparative literature rarely reports both under a controlled, shared
        protocol [9, 10].
●​      Data-Driven Model Suitability: Forecasting performance depends critically on data
        characteristics such as continuity, sparsity, and scale heterogeneity, and the
        behaviour of Transformer-family architectures on industrial data exhibiting these
        properties remains largely unexamined in the reviewed literature [11, 12].
●​      Accuracy–Efficiency Trade-offs: Transformers frequently report superior accuracy
        on curated benchmarks, while recurrent architectures retain clear advantages in
        training speed, memory footprint, and parameter efficiency; explicit, quantified
        guidance on navigating this trade-off remains limited [9, 10].
●​      Failure-Mode Documentation: Instances in which state-of-the-art architectures fail

                                              31
       outright, as opposed to merely underperforming, are rarely documented with
       sufficient diagnostic detail to support practical risk assessment, despite the surveys
       reviewed in Section 2.4.6 explicitly identifying this as an open gap [34, 35].
     This thesis addresses these gaps directly by conducting a controlled, multi-dataset
evaluation of three Transformer architectures (Informer, Autoformer, PatchTST) alongside
four recurrent baselines (LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and a minimal single-layer LSTM
configuration), executed under a single, explicitly documented hardware budget
representative of constrained, non-server-class deployment. Through joint quantitative
benchmarking, qualitative prediction-fidelity analysis, and a detailed failure analysis on an
industrial energy dataset exhibiting the extreme scale heterogeneity and discontinuous
behaviour   identified   as   underexamined       above, the   study provides     actionable,
evidence-based guidance for both researchers and practitioners navigating the expanding
landscape of time-series forecasting architectures.

3.1​Overview

     This chapter presents, in full detail, the research methodology used to investigate the
comparative performance of Transformer-based and recurrent forecasting architectures for
long-term time series forecasting (LTSF), with a particular focus on electricity load
forecasting under realistic hardware constraints. It documents the datasets selected and their
statistical properties, the preprocessing and feature-engineering pipeline applied uniformly
to them, the internal mechanics of each of the seven models evaluated — including their
mathematical formulation and, for every architecture, an accompanying structural diagram
— the training protocol followed, and the evaluation framework used to compare predictive
accuracy against computational cost. The chapter follows a structured, reproducible
approach intended to ensure that the results reported in Chapter 4 are robust, internally
consistent, and relevant to real-world deployment conditions rather than artefacts of an
uncontrolled experimental design.
     The central objective of this study is to assess the practical viability of three
Transformer-based architectures — Informer, Autoformer, and PatchTST [7, 8, 9] —
against four recurrent baselines — LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and a deliberately minimal
single-layer LSTM configuration [5, 21, 22] — for electricity load forecasting. The
research places particular emphasis on the trade-off between predictive accuracy, model
complexity, and computational efficiency, and on how this trade-off is affected by the
constrained hardware budgets typical of small research groups and edge deployments,
rather than the server-class hardware assumed in much of the published LTSF literature [9,
10]. All experiments reported in this thesis were executed on a single ASUS ROG Zephyrus
G14 (GA401IV) laptop equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2060 (Max-Q, 6GB
                                             33
VRAM), a configuration chosen specifically to represent this class of constrained,
non-server-class hardware.
     The experimental design follows a systematic, staged approach. Four publicly
available datasets, spanning meteorological, industrial, and residential domains, were
selected to test model generalizability across a range of statistical regularity — from the
smooth, continuous behaviour of weather data to the extreme scale heterogeneity and
discontinuity of industrial energy data [12]. After a unified preprocessing and
feature-engineering pipeline was applied, all seven models were trained under matched
conditions on each dataset, with predictive accuracy (MAE, MSE, RMSE) reported jointly
with operational cost (training time, parameter count, and VRAM occupancy), consistent
with the hardware-aware evaluation framework motivated in Chapters 1 and 2 [10, 11].
     A methodological principle that runs throughout this chapter, and that distinguishes
this study from much of the comparative LTSF literature, is that architectural description
and empirical configuration are kept explicitly separate. Where a structural diagram in this
chapter illustrates a model's general mechanism using representative input dimensions
drawn from a reference implementation, this is stated plainly, and the reader is directed to
the actual experimental configuration used in this thesis — detailed fully in Section 3.5 and
Section 3.7 — for the precise Lookback, Horizon, and batch-size values used to generate
the results reported in Chapter 4. This separation is necessary because illustrative shapes
convenient for explaining a mechanism (for instance, a short lookback window that fits
legibly in a diagram) are not always identical to the values used in full-scale
experimentation on 6GB VRAM hardware.

    3.2.1 The Weather (WTH) Dataset
           The Weather (WTH) dataset contains hourly meteorological records comprising
    35,064 total entries across 12 variables, including temperature, humidity, air pressure, wind
    speed, and visibility. In this research, WTH served as the calibration dataset used to
    determine a stable training configuration and early-stopping window before committing to
    more computationally expensive runs on the remaining datasets. Because WTH is smaller
    and less noisy than the electricity datasets, it allowed rapid iteration: by comparing model
    convergence behaviour over 5 and 10 training epochs, the study established the epoch
    budget and early-stopping patience used in subsequent experiments, described further in
    Section 3.5. The dataset's smooth, continuous, and strongly periodic character — diurnal
    temperature cycles and seasonal humidity trends being the dominant sources of structure —
    also makes it a useful sanity check: any architecture that fails to converge stably on WTH
    would raise concerns about the correctness of the implementation itself, rather than about
    the architecture's suitability for harder data, and no such failure was observed for any of the
    seven models evaluated.
    3.2.2 The ETTh1 (Electricity Transformer Temperature) Dataset
           The ETT family of datasets was derived from electrical power grid transformers
    monitored in China over a two-year period, and is split into hourly (ETTh1, ETTh2) and
    15-minute (ETTm1, ETTm2) variants sharing the same multivariate structure of six load
    factors and one oil-temperature target [7]. This study uses ETTh1, containing 17,420 hourly

                                                    36
samples in its canonical form, chosen over the minute-level variants because its lower
sampling frequency substantially reduces the memory overhead imposed by the quadratic
scaling of self-attention with sequence length [1], while still providing 24 data points per
day of context for the Lookback-96 window used throughout this thesis.
     ETTh1 is used in this research as a canonical benchmark against which the training
pipeline's technical correctness can be validated prior to evaluation on more volatile data.
Its native structure comprises an hourly timestamp, six exogenous load variables (HUFL,
HULL, MUFL, MULL, LUFL, LULL), and a single target variable, Oil Temperature (OT),
which the models are trained to forecast 24 steps ahead. As detailed in Section 3.3, this
study's feature-engineering pipeline expands the native seven-column structure to 33 input
features per timestep through lagged values, rolling statistics, and cyclical calendar
encodings; this expansion is disclosed explicitly here because it constitutes a
methodological departure from the seven-feature configuration used in the original
Informer, Autoformer, and PatchTST publications, a point revisited when this thesis's
results are compared against those publications' own reported benchmarks in Chapter 4.
     The dataset's strong 24-hour and 7-day periodicity, combined with clear correlation
between load variables and the target, makes it well suited to Transformer architectures
whose self-attention mechanisms can directly relate observations separated by exactly these
periodic intervals. Because ETTh1 is comparatively well-behaved and continuous, it also
serves as a control condition: strong performance here establishes a baseline of confidence
that is then contrasted against the STEEL dataset described next, where that continuity
assumption is deliberately violated.
3.2.3 The Steel Industry Energy Consumption Dataset
     To test the robustness of the evaluated architectures under conditions that depart
substantially from curated forecasting benchmarks, this study incorporates the Steel
Industry Energy Consumption dataset [12]. Unlike WTH and ETTh1, industrial energy data
of this kind is characterised by high volatility, abrupt machinery on/off cycles, and complex
multivariate interactions rather than smooth periodicity. The dataset comprises 35,040
records at 15-minute resolution across 11 variables, including real-time energy usage
(Usage\_kWh), lagging and leading reactive power, CO₂ emissions, and a categorical
load-type indicator (Light, Medium, or Maximum load); of these, seven continuous
                                             37
variables were retained for modelling after feature selection.
     As documented in the data-loading logs for this study, the raw feature scales in this
dataset are markedly heterogeneous: energy usage ranges from 0 to 157.18 kWh (mean
27.39, std 33.44), while the seconds-since-midnight (NSM) feature ranges from 0 to 85,500
(mean 42,750, std 24,940.53) and the CO₂ channel ranges from 0 to only 0.07 (mean 0.01)
— a span of roughly six to seven orders of magnitude across the seven variables retained
after feature selection. Because industrial machinery can be switched on or off effectively
instantaneously, the resulting load profile also exhibits sharp, discontinuous vertical
transitions rather than the gradual trends typical of environmental or residential data. This
dataset therefore serves in this research as a deliberate stress test, designed to determine
whether the architectural advantages reported for Transformer models on curated
benchmarks persist under scale heterogeneity and discontinuity of this kind; the results of
this stress test, including a documented and mechanistically refined failure analysis, are
reported in Chapter 4.
3.2.4 The Smart Meters in London Dataset
     To evaluate model performance in a practical, large-scale urban context, this research
incorporates the Smart Meters in London dataset, provided by UK Power Networks under
the Low Carbon London programme [36]. The dataset contains half-hourly electricity
consumption readings for a sample of London households collected between November
2011 and February 2014, enriched in this study's processed form with weather, household,
and calendar/holiday covariates, yielding 26 input features. At over 3.4 million raw
readings prior to windowing, this is by a wide margin the largest dataset used in this thesis
— roughly two orders of magnitude larger than ETTh1 — and it serves as the primary
practical-application benchmark for comparing Transformer-based models against the
recurrent baselines under realistic residential load conditions, including genuine
household-level noise such as localised consumption spikes from individual appliances.
The scale of this dataset also has direct methodological consequences documented in
Section 3.5: at this volume, a single Transformer training epoch can require close to an hour
of wall-clock time on the target hardware, which necessitated an explicit, disclosed epoch
cap rather than full convergence to early-stopping criteria for this dataset alone.

3.3​Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

     A unified preprocessing and feature-engineering pipeline was applied to all four
datasets to ensure that comparisons between architectures reflect genuine model differences
rather than inconsistencies in data preparation. Given the specific challenges of time-series
data, this pipeline was designed with particular care to preserve chronological structure
while addressing missing values, distributional shift, and scale heterogeneity.
     Missing values, which constituted a small proportion of the total data in every dataset
used, were handled via linear interpolation, preserving the smooth transition of the
underlying signal without introducing the step artefacts that simple forward-fill methods
can produce. Following this, all continuous features were normalised using Z-score
standardisation via scikit-learn's StandardScaler [37]:
                            z = (x − μ) / σ
     where μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of each feature computed on the
training partition only, and then applied unmodified to the validation and test partitions to
avoid information leakage. As discussed in Chapter 4, this standardisation proved sufficient
for the WTH, ETTh1, and Smart Meters datasets, but was, on its own, insufficient to
prevent numerical instability in two of the three Transformer architectures on the STEEL
dataset, a finding examined in detail in the results chapter.
     The data was then divided chronologically into training, validation, and test partitions
in a ratio of 70% / 20% / 10%, consistent across all four datasets and confirmed directly by
the data-loader shapes recorded during experimentation (for example, ETTh1 yields 12,177
training, 3,479 validation, and 1,740 test sequences, an exact 70/20/10 split of the dataset's
17,396 usable windows). This chronological, non-shuffled split is essential for time-series
evaluation: because the split preserves temporal order, the model is always evaluated on
data strictly later than anything seen during training, preventing look-ahead bias and
mirroring the realistic forecasting scenario in which future information is unavailable at
training time [43].
     Feature engineering was applied to help each model capture temporal structure more
directly. Calendar features (hour of day, day of week, month) were encoded using
sine–cosine cyclical transformations of the form sin(2πt/T) and cos(2πt/T), where t is the

                                               39
feature's raw value and T its period (for example, T = 24 for hour-of-day), ensuring that the
23rd hour is represented as numerically adjacent to the 0th hour rather than as a
discontinuous jump [4]. For the datasets richest in exogenous structure — ETTh1, STEEL
and Smart Meters — lagged values (at 1, 6, 12, and 24-hour intervals) and rolling-window
statistics (mean and standard deviation over 3-, 12-, and 24-hour windows) were
additionally computed, providing the models with explicit short-term trend and volatility
markers rather than requiring these to be inferred purely from raw sequences. This
engineering step is what expands ETTh1 from its native 7 columns to the 33 input features
confirmed in the data-loader logs, and is disclosed here as a specific, quantified
methodological choice rather than an implicit detail.
     All datasets were then arranged into fixed-length input–output windows using a
Lookback of 96 timesteps, a Label length of 48 timesteps, and a prediction Horizon of 24
timesteps, consistent across every model and dataset in this study and confirmed directly in
the corresponding data-loader initialisation logs. The Label length overlaps with the final
48 steps of the Lookback window and is provided to the Transformer decoders as a “start
token” containing recent ground-truth context, following the generative decoding scheme
used by Informer and Autoformer, while the recurrent models and PatchTST consume only
the Lookback window directly.


3.4​Comparative Model Architectures and Core Mechanics

     This research evaluates two families of sequence-modelling architecture —
Transformer-based models and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) — which differ
fundamentally in how they process temporal information. Transformers process the entire
input sequence in parallel via self-attention, computing relationships between arbitrary pairs
of timesteps directly; RNNs instead process the sequence step by step, propagating
information forward through an evolving hidden state [1, 5]. For every architecture
discussed in this section, a structural diagram is provided to make its internal data flow
explicit, in keeping with the presentation style of the original thesis on which this chapter is
based.




                                              40
3.4.1​ Transformer-Based Models

     The three Transformer variants evaluated in this study — Informer, Autoformer, and
PatchTST — each adapt the standard multi-head self-attention meta-architecture to address
its quadratic time and memory complexity with respect to sequence length, while pursuing
different strategies for capturing time-series-specific structure. Before examining each
variant individually, it is useful to recall the general Transformer meta-architecture from
which all three descend.
     The Standard Transformer Meta-Architecture
     The vanilla Transformer, illustrated in Figure 3.1, follows an encoder–decoder
structure in which both encoder and decoder are composed of Nx identical stacked blocks
[1]. Each encoder block consists of a multi-head self-attention module followed by a
position-wise feed-forward network, with residual (“Add”) connections and layer
normalisation applied around each sub-layer; each decoder block additionally inserts a
masked multi-head self-attention module and a cross-attention module that attends over the
encoder's output. Positional encoding is added to both the input and output embeddings,
since the architecture itself contains no recurrence and would otherwise be blind to
sequence order. The output of the final decoder block is projected through a linear layer
and, in the original language-modelling formulation, a softmax to produce output
probabilities; in the time-series forecasting adaptations used in this thesis, this final
projection instead produces continuous-valued forecasts directly.




                                             41
                 Figure 3.2: Standard Transformer Encoder–Decoder Architecture

    The core computational primitive underlying every block is scaled dot-product
attention over a Query-Key-Value (QKV) representation:
                   Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √d ) V
    where Q, K, and V are learned linear projections of the input, and d             is the
dimensionality of the key vectors, used to rescale the dot product before the softmax to
avoid vanishingly small gradients. Multi-head attention computes several such attention
functions in parallel over different learned projections of the input, allowing the model to
attend to different representation subspaces simultaneously. It is this mechanism's O(L²)
time and memory cost with respect to sequence length L that motivated the three
                                              42
efficiency-oriented variants examined next.

Informer: ProbSparse Attention and Self-Attention Distilling
     The Informer model represents a pivotal advancement in the Long-term Time Series
                                                                         2
Forecasting (LSTF) landscape, specifically designed to mitigate the 𝑂(𝐿 ) time complexity
and memory usage of the vanilla Transformer. Its architecture is built upon the observation
that the distribution of self-attention is often sparse, meaning only a few dot-product pairs
contribute significantly to the overall attention mechanism. To exploit this, Informer
introduces the ProbSparse Self-Attention mechanism. This technique utilizes a
"Query-Sparsity" measurement to identify the most dominant queries, allowing the model
to compute attention only for these high-probability pairs while ignoring the "lazy" or
redundant ones. This mathematical refinement successfully reduces the computational
burden to O(L log L)[7] enabling the model to handle much longer lookback windows
without                   exhausting                     hardware                   resource




                          Figure 3.3: ProbSparse Attention Mechanism

     Beyond its attention mechanism, the Informer utilizes a specialized encoder-decoder
structure to streamline the forecasting process. The encoder implements Self-Attention
Distilling, a process that applies 1D convolutions and max-pooling between attention
layers. This "distills" the hidden state by halving its temporal dimension at each step,
ensuring the model focuses only on the most critical temporal features while maintaining a
manageable memory footprint. On the receiving end, the decoder is designed for Generative
                                              43
Inference, which stands in stark contrast to the recursive, step-by-step prediction methods
of traditional RNNs.

     In this study's implementation, Informer's parameter count varied by dataset according

approximately 2.90 million parameters on WTH and ETTh1 down to 87,623 parameters
under the deliberately reduced-capacity configuration used for STEEL and 27,610
parameters under the memory-optimised configuration used for the much larger Smart
Meters dataset.
Autoformer: Series Decomposition and Auto-Correlation
     The Autoformer architecture represents a paradigm shift in long-term forecasting by
moving beyond simple attention mechanisms to a "decomposition-based" approach.
Traditional Transformers often struggle with time series because they treat the sequence as
a flat set of tokens, ignoring the underlying structural components like long-term trends and
seasonal fluctuations. Autoformer addresses this by embedding a Series Decomposition
Block directly into its architecture. This block continuously separates the input hidden
                                              44
states into two distinct paths: the Trend-Cyclical component, which captures the long-term
progression of the data, and the Seasonal component, which captures the repeating,
high-frequency patterns. This internal separation allows the model to handle the inherent
non-stationarity of energy datasets like ETTh1 and Smart London more effectively than
vanilla architectures.




                   Figure 3.5: Autoformer Architecture with Series Decomposition

     The second core innovation of the Autoformer is its replacement of the standard
dot-product self-attention with the Auto-Correlation Mechanism. While standard attention
calculates point-to-point similarities—a process that is both computationally expensive and
susceptible to noise—Auto-Correlation identifies sub-series level similarities. It utilizes the
Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to calculate autocorrelation in the frequency domain,
allowing the model to discover period-based dependencies. By aggregating similar
sub-series segments across different periods, the Auto-Correlation mechanism provides a
much stronger inductive bias for time series data. This mechanism reduces the
computational complexity to O(L log L) [8] while significantly improving the model's
ability to capture the multi-seasonal rhythms found in residential and industrial load
profiles.




                                                45
              Figure 3.6: Auto-Correlation Mechanism and Period-Based Dependencies

     In our experimental results, the Autoformer demonstrated a high degree of predictive
stability, particularly in capturing the cyclical trends of the ETTh1 dataset. However, this
structural complexity comes with a trade-off in efficiency. As illustrated in our comparative
training time analysis, the multiple decomposition stages and the frequency-domain
calculations resulted in the longest training duration among all tested models, exceeding
68,000 seconds. While the Autoformer is an exceptionally powerful tool for capturing
complex, repeating patterns, its deployment in resource-constrained or real-time
environments requires significant computational overhead compared to more streamlined
models like PatchTST.
PatchTST: Sub-series Patching and Channel-Independent Processing
     PatchTST (Patch Time Series Transformer) represents the most significant departure
from the point-wise processing paradigm found in earlier Transformer models. While the
Informer and Autoformer treat individual time steps as tokens, PatchTST introduces the
concept of Sub-series Patching. In this approach, the input sequence is segmented into
overlapping or non-overlapping patches—for example, grouping 16 consecutive time steps
into a single patch with a stride of 8. This method effectively transforms the input from a
long, thin vector into a series of information-dense tokens. By focusing on patches rather
than points, the model can capture local semantic patterns that are often lost in the noise of
                                                                                            2
individual observations, while simultaneously reducing the attention complexity from O(𝐿 )
       2
to O (𝑁 ), where (𝑁 )is the significantly smaller number of patches.



                                              46
                   Figure 3.7: Sub-series Patching and Sequence Segmentation

    Complementing the patching layer is the principle of Channel Independence. In many
multivariate time-series tasks, different sensors or variables (such as Temperature vs.
Electricity Load) may have vastly different scales or patterns. Traditional models often
attempt to flatten these into a single embedding, which can lead to feature interference.
PatchTST instead processes each variable as an independent channel. Each channel is fed
through the same Transformer backbone with shared weights, which allows the model to
learn universal temporal patterns while maintaining the unique signal integrity of each
specific variable. This independence is particularly crucial for the Smart London dataset,
where residential anomalies in one household's consumption should not be allowed to
corrupt the broader community load profile.




                                              47
                      Figure 3.8: Patching and Temporal Feature Extraction

     The overall architecture of PatchTST utilizes a standard Transformer Encoder but
applies it to these extracted patches. Following the encoding process, a simple linear head is
used for the Prediction Block, mapping the patch representations directly to the target
Horizon 24. This design proved to be the most efficient in our research; because the
effective sequence length is reduced by the patching factor, the model consumes far less
VRAM and trains significantly faster than the Autoformer. As a result, PatchTST achieved
the best performance in our empirical analysis, producing an MSE of 18.38 and
demonstrating that "less is more" when it comes to token granularity in long-term

     These models, based on the Transformer architecture, shift the paradigm toward better
efficiency compared to the earlier model versions, while the achieved predictive accuracy
remains at a comparable level. The capability of parallel processing for long sequences and
handling large data with minimal computational overhead is an important advantage in
performing forecasting tasks under resource-constrained conditions.

3.4.2​ Recurrent Neural Network Baselines

     In contrast to the parallel processing of Transformer architectures, the four recurrent
baselines evaluated in this study process sequences step by step, maintaining an internal
hidden state that carries information forward through time [5]. Figure 3.4 illustrates the
basic recurrent computational unit common to this entire family: at each timestep t, the
current input xₜ is combined with the hidden state carried forward from the previous
timestep and passed through a nonlinearity — typically tanh — to produce the updated
hidden state hₜ, which is both output and propagated forward to the next timestep.




                                             49
                        Figure 3.10: Basic Recurrent Neural Network Unit

     A plain RNN of this form is prone to the vanishing and exploding gradient problem
when trained via backpropagation through time over long sequences, because the same
weight matrix is repeatedly multiplied through the unrolled computational graph. This
limitation, well documented in the literature [41], is the direct motivation for the gated
architectures described in the remainder of this section.
LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)
     The LSTM addresses the vanishing-gradient problem through a gated memory-cell
design comprising three gates, each implemented as a sigmoid-activated linear layer over
the concatenation of the current input and previous hidden state: a Forget Gate, which
determines what proportion of the existing cell state to retain; an Input Gate, which
determines what proportion of a candidate update (computed via a tanh layer) to add to the
cell state; and an Output Gate, which determines what proportion of the (tanh-activated)
cell state to expose as the new hidden state [5]. Figure 3.5 illustrates this internal gating
structure.



                                              50
                           Figure 3.11: LSTM Cell and Gating Structure

     A defining property of this gating structure, directly relevant to the failure-mode
analysis in Chapter 4, is that every gate is sigmoid-activated (bounding its output to the
range [0, 1]) and every content update is tanh-activated (bounding its output to [−1, 1]).
This means that, regardless of the magnitude of any single input value, its contribution to
the cell state and hidden state at each timestep is mathematically bounded. This
sophisticated mechanism enables the LSTM to learn the medium-to-long-range
dependencies essential for tracking the daily load cycles in datasets like ETTh1, and it
remains a widely used and computationally efficient baseline for load forecasting [6].
BiLSTM (Bidirectional LSTM)
     The BiLSTM extends the standard LSTM by processing the input sequence in both
temporal directions using two parallel LSTM layers — a Forward Layer processing the
sequence from start to end, and a Backward Layer processing it from end to start — and
concatenating their hidden states at each timestep, as illustrated in Figure 3.6. This provides
the model with both past and future context within the input window at every point in the
sequence, which can improve representational richness for dependencies that are not purely
                                               51
causal.




                       Figure 3.12: Bidirectional LSTM Information Flow

     This richer representation typically comes at approximately double the parameter
count and training time relative to a unidirectional LSTM of the same hidden size, since
two full sets of gating parameters must be learned and both directions' computations must
be performed at every timestep. This trade-off is examined empirically in Chapter 4, where
BiLSTM's accuracy relative to plain LSTM varies by dataset.
GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)
     The GRU simplifies the LSTM's three-gate structure into two gates — a Reset Gate
and an Update Gate — and merges the cell state and hidden state into a single vector, as
shown in Figure 3.7. The Reset Gate determines how much of the previous hidden state to
disregard when computing a candidate update (via a tanh layer), while the Update Gate
determines the interpolation between the previous hidden state and this candidate to
produce the new hidden state, implemented through the (1 − z) and z weighting shown in
the diagram.




                                             52
                            Figure 3.13: GRU Cell and Gating Structure

     Like the LSTM, every gate in the GRU is sigmoid-activated and every content update
is tanh-activated, preserving the same bounded-activation property discussed above.
Because it has fewer parameters than the LSTM, the GRU is computationally lighter and
often converges faster, making it particularly well suited to scenarios where training
efficiency   is   a   priority;   as   shown    in   Chapter    4,   GRU offered a     strong
accuracy-per-parameter trade-off across all four datasets evaluated in this study, and was in
some cases the single most accurate recurrent model tested.
     The Minimal Single-Layer LSTM Configuration
     To establish an explicit lower bound on model capacity within the recurrent family,
this study additionally evaluates a deliberately minimal, single-layer LSTM configuration
with a hidden dimension of 16 units — substantially smaller than the 32–64-unit hidden
dimensions typically used for the LSTM and GRU baselines above — whose final hidden
state is projected directly to the output horizon without additional intermediate layers. This
configuration, denoted EfficientLSTM in the accompanying experiment logs and referred to
as LSTM-16 in Chapters 4 and 5, shares the identical internal gating structure illustrated in
Figure 3.5 and is treated in this thesis as a capacity-reduced variant of the standard LSTM
                                               53
architecture rather than a novel design; its role is specifically to quantify the accuracy
sacrificed at the smallest practical parameter footprint. Across the datasets evaluated, its
parameter count ranged from 4,456 (STEEL) to 13,424 (Smart Meters), consistently the
smallest of any model tested. As reported in Chapter 4, this configuration was competitive
with the other three recurrent baselines on the smaller, seven-feature STEEL dataset, but
was the least accurate model of all seven evaluated on the larger, twenty-six-feature Smart
Meters dataset — direct evidence that a fixed minimal capacity is adequate for some
forecasting tasks and inadequate for others, rather than being unconditionally sufficient or
insufficient.

3.5​Training Approaches

     The training strategy was designed to enable a fair, reproducible comparison of all
seven models while remaining feasible within the 6GB VRAM budget described in Section
3.1. Training proceeded in two stages: an initial calibration stage on the WTH dataset to
establish a stable epoch budget, followed by a final training stage applied consistently
across all four datasets.
3.5.1 Initial Calibration on the WTH Dataset
     The first stage of training was conducted on the WTH dataset specifically because its
smaller size allowed rapid iteration. All models were trained separately, and their
validation-loss trajectories over 5 and 10 epochs were compared to determine an
appropriate epoch budget and early-stopping patience. This calibration surfaced an
important methodological finding, detailed further in Chapter 4: at 5 epochs, Informer
achieved better summary error statistics than Autoformer despite producing visibly flatter,
less temporally faithful predictions, indicating that a short training budget alone was
insufficient to distinguish genuine pattern learning from a low-error but uninformative
mean-reverting prediction. This motivated the adoption of a 10-epoch training budget, with
early-stopping patience of 5–7 epochs depending on model family, for all subsequent
experiments.
3.5.2 Final Training Across Datasets
     With the epoch budget established, all seven models were trained on each of the four
datasets under a matched protocol. The Adam optimiser [38] was used throughout, with a
                                            54
learning rate of 1×10⁻⁴ for the three Transformer models and 1×10⁻³ for the four recurrent
models, values chosen to reflect the differing training stability characteristics of each family
[39]. Early stopping monitored validation loss with a patience of 5 epochs for the recurrent
models and 7 epochs for the Transformer models [40], halting training once no further
improvement was observed and thereby avoiding unnecessary computation once a model
had converged or, in the case of the STEEL dataset's Informer and Autoformer runs, once
no meaningful validation signal was present at all.
     Batch size was set to 32 for all four recurrent models across every dataset. For the
Transformer models, batch size was constrained by the 6GB VRAM budget and varied by
dataset according to model size and input dimensionality: a batch size of 8 was used for the
Transformer models on WTH, ETTh1, and STEEL, while a batch size of 16 was used on
the substantially smaller-capacity Transformer configurations trained on the Smart Meters
dataset. This dataset-dependent adjustment is reported here explicitly, in line with the
hardware-aware framing of this thesis, rather than treated as a hidden methodological
detail: it reflects a genuine, disclosed consequence of training under a fixed memory budget
rather than an inconsistency in experimental design.
3.5.3 Model-Specific Training Considerations
     Each architecture imposed distinct practical constraints on training. The Transformer
models' memory consumption scales with the product of sequence length and model
dimension due to the self-attention mechanism, which required their internal dimensions to
be reduced substantially on the STEEL and Smart Meters datasets relative to WTH and
ETTh1 in order to remain within the available VRAM; PatchTST's patch-based
tokenisation made it consistently the least memory-intensive of the three Transformers
under every configuration tested. The recurrent models required comparatively little
adjustment across datasets, with hidden dimensions of 16–64 units sufficient in every case,
reflecting their more modest, linear-in-sequence-length memory footprint.
     Training duration and convergence behaviour also differed substantially between
families. Across all datasets on which training was stable, the Transformer models required
considerably more wall-clock time than the recurrent baselines to complete an equivalent
number of epochs — for instance, on the Smart Meters dataset, Informer and Autoformer
required 11,119.1 and 10,694.7 seconds respectively to complete three epochs, against
                                              55
1,189.7–1,592.2 seconds for the four recurrent models to complete the same three epochs, a
difference of roughly one order of magnitude attributable primarily to the larger per-batch
attention computation and the smaller batch sizes necessitated by VRAM constraints. Each
model's final evaluation was conducted on the held-out test partition only after its validation
loss had stabilised or its early-stopping criterion had triggered, and training time, parameter
count, and peak memory occupancy were recorded alongside predictive accuracy for every
run, consistent with the joint accuracy–efficiency evaluation framework introduced in
Chapter 1.


3.6​Evaluation Metrics

     Consistent with the joint accuracy–efficiency framework motivating this thesis, model
evaluation is bifurcated into statistical accuracy metrics and computational efficiency
benchmarks; neither is treated as sufficient on its own to characterise a model's practical
suitability for deployment.
3.6.1 Statistical Accuracy Metrics
     Given a set of n test-set predictions ŷᵢ and corresponding ground-truth values yᵢ, the
following three metrics are computed:
●​     Mean Absolute Error (MAE) = (1/n) Σ |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|: the average magnitude of prediction
       error in the original units of the target variable, providing a directly interpretable
       measure of typical deviation [42].
●​     Mean Squared Error (MSE) = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²: the average squared prediction error,
       which penalises large deviations disproportionately and is therefore sensitive to the
       occasional large errors that matter most in operational load-forecasting contexts
       [42].
●​     Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) = √MSE: the square root of MSE, returning the
       error to the original units of the target variable while retaining sensitivity to large
       deviations.
     Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) was also computed during experimentation
but is reported only where meaningful: on the Smart Meters dataset, MAPE returned an
infinite or undefined value for every model tested, a data artefact caused by exact zero
values in the target series triggering division by zero, and is therefore excluded from the
                                              56
comparative tables in Chapter 4 in favour of the three metrics above.
3.6.2 Computational and Operational Metrics
●​     Training Time: total wall-clock time in seconds to reach the early-stopping criterion
       or the epoch budget, used to assess the practical cost of achieving a given level of
       accuracy on the target hardware.
●​     Parameter Count: the total number of trainable parameters in each model
       configuration, used as a hardware-independent measure of model complexity and
       memory footprint.
●​     GPU Memory Usage (VRAM): peak memory occupancy during training, directly
       relevant given the 6GB VRAM budget of the hardware used throughout this study,
       and the metric most responsible for the dataset-dependent batch-size and
       model-dimension adjustments described in Section 3.5.

3.8​Conclusion of Methodological Approach

     IThis methodology was designed to bridge the gap between architectural innovation
and practical deployment feasibility. By subjecting seven models — from the high-capacity
Autoformer to the deliberately minimal single-layer LSTM configuration — to identical
preprocessing,   a   matched    (though,   where    necessary,   explicitly   disclosed   and
dataset-conditional) training protocol, and a consistent joint accuracy–efficiency evaluation
framework across four datasets of varying statistical regularity, this study provides a
transparent and reproducible basis for the model-selection guidance developed in later
chapters.
     Critically, this methodology does not treat hardware constraints or data irregularity as
sources of noise to be minimised, but as first-class experimental variables in their own
right. By explicitly recording and reporting parameter counts, training times, and VRAM
occupancy alongside predictive accuracy for every model and dataset combination, and by
including the STEEL dataset specifically to test the limits of the standard LTSF pipeline
under conditions of extreme scale heterogeneity, this research is positioned to explain not
only which architecture performs best, but under what data and hardware conditions that
ranking holds — and where it breaks down entirely. The results of applying this
methodology are presented in full in Chapter 4.

cycles, as detailed in Chapter 3.
●​     Weather (WTH): 35,064 hourly meteorological records, used primarily for initial

●​     ETTh1 (Electricity Transformer Temperature): 17,420 hourly records, an industrial

4.2​ Preprocessing and Feature Engineering

     A unified preprocessing pipeline was applied to all datasets to ensure methodological
consistency and enable a fair comparison between Transformer-based and RNN-based
architectures. The process began with the maintenance of temporal integrity; missing
values—constituting less than 0.5% of the total data—were handled via linear interpolation
to preserve the smooth transition of signals without introducing the "step" artifacts often
caused by simple fill methods. To achieve numerical homogeneity across variables with
varying scales (such as temperature in Celsius vs. energy load in kW), all continuous
features underwent Z-score normalization using StandardScaler. This transformation
aligned the distributions to a mean of zero and unit variance, a step that proved
indispensable for stabilizing gradient flow and preventing scale-induced collapse in the
attention-based models.
     Feature engineering further enriched the models by capturing complex temporal
dynamics. Temporal features were encoded using sine-cosine cyclical transformations,
which translate calendar markers (hour, day, month) into a continuous circular space,
allowing the models to recognize that the 23rd hour is adjacent to the 0th hour.

                                              60
Additionally, the pipeline integrated lag features (at 1, 6, 12, and 24-hour intervals) and
rolling statistics, such as moving means and standard deviations. These additions provided
the models with explicit markers for local trends and volatility, significantly improving
prediction stability in high-variance regimes like the STEEL dataset.
4.2.1 PatchTST Mechanics: Sub-series Patching
     A core innovation focused on in this research is the Sub-series Patching mechanism
utilized by PatchTST. Unlike earlier models like Informer, which treat individual time steps

as isolated tokens, PatchTST segments the input sequence of length 𝐿 into smaller,

overlapping patches of length 𝑃 using a defined stride 𝑆 . For instance, by grouping 16
time steps into a single patch with a stride of 4, the model transforms a high-resolution
sequence into a series of information-dense tokens.
     This segmentation process offers two primary advantages. First, it retains local
semantic information—the "shape" of a trend—that is often lost in the point-wise noise of
standard observations. Second, it mathematically reduces the computational complexity of
                                          2          2
the self-attention mechanism from O(𝐿 ) to O(𝑁 ), where 𝑁 is the significantly smaller
number of patches. This reduction is what allows PatchTST to maintain a remarkably low
VRAM footprint even when processing long lookback windows.

                                               61
4.2.2 Channel Independence and Model Backbone
     Beyond the fundamental shift of patching, PatchTST implements Channel
Independence, a paradigm shift in multivariate time-series forecasting. In traditional
models, multiple features—such as Temperature, Electricity Load, and Voltage—are often
concatenated or embedded into a single vector, which can lead to "feature interference." In
such cases, a significant anomaly or high-variance noise in one variable (e.g., a sudden
industrial power spike) can inadvertently corrupt the signal integrity of more stable
variables. PatchTST mitigates this by treating each multivariate feature as an independent
channel. Each channel is processed through the same Transformer backbone using shared
weights, allowing the model to learn universal temporal patterns while maintaining the
unique structural characteristics of each individual variable.
     The end-to-end architectural journey begins with Reversible Instance Normalization
(RevIN), a critical component designed to address the non-stationary nature of energy data
by handling distribution shifts between training and testing sets. Once normalized, the
sequence is segmented into patches and fed into the core TSTiEncoder (the backbone). This
encoder utilizes the standard Transformer blocks—comprising multi-head self-attention and
position-wise feed-forward networks—to extract deep, high-level temporal dependencies
from the patches. This stage is visualized in the model's structural overview, illustrating the

high-level flow from input tensor 𝑋 to the predicted output.




                                              62
                     Figure 4.2: PatchTST Architecture and End-to-End Flow

     The internal mechanics of the backbone are further detailed through a hierarchical
processing structure. After the TSTiEncoder processes the independent channels, the
resulting multidimensional representations must be translated back into a tangible forecast.
This is handled by the FlattenHead Layer, where the latent data is flattened and passed
through a linear projection head to produce the final 24-step generative forecast. Unlike the
recursive,   step-by-step   prediction    methods     used    in    traditional   RNNs,   this
"Backbone-to-Head" flow generates the entire horizon in a single forward pass. This
prevents the "cascading error" effect, where a small mistake in the first predicted hour
grows into a significant deviation by the end of the window. The granular view of this
backbone highlights the seamless integration of RevIN, Patching, and the TSTiEncoder,
ensuring that the model remains both computationally efficient and highly accurate.

calibration-stage-only impression that PatchTST was unconditionally the fastest

        This second run resolves a question left open by the first: because the
smallest-capacity model in the entire comparison (LSTM-16, at 4,456 parameters)
succeeds, while a larger model (Informer, at 87,623 parameters) fails, raw model capacity
cannot be the explanation for the collapse. An explanation based purely on channel-mixing
in general is similarly ruled out, because every recurrent model processes all seven STEEL
variables jointly, concatenated into a single input vector at each timestep, and none of them
fail.
        The evidence instead points to a specific, previously under-specified mechanism: a
bounded-activation explanation. As established architecturally in Chapter 3, every gate in
the LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and LSTM-16 cells is sigmoid-activated, and every candidate
content update is tanh-activated — mathematically bounding the contribution of any single
input value to a fixed numerical range, regardless of how many channels are combined at
that timestep. Self-attention and auto-correlation, by contrast, compute raw dot-product or
correlation scores with no such bound. A heavy-tailed value that survives Z-score
standardisation in one channel (STEEL's raw features span roughly six to seven orders of
                                               74
magnitude before scaling) can therefore dominate a computation that mixes all channels
together without any bound on its contribution, in exactly the manner that produces the
exploding values consistent with NaN propagation [41]. PatchTST avoids this specific
failure mode through channel independence: because its attention is computed only within
each channel's own patches, an outlier value in one channel cannot inflate the attention
computation carried out for a different channel. This refines the channel-independence
hypothesis explored in Chapter 2 [11]: the discriminating property is not channel-mixing in
general — which every successful recurrent model in this comparison performs routinely
— but specifically whether that mixing occurs through an unbounded, cross-channel
attention or correlation computation.
     This refined mechanism directly supersedes an earlier, less precise account offered at
an initial stage of this research, which attributed the STEEL failure to softmax
exponentiation exceeding the floating-point representable range [44]. While floating-point
overflow of this kind is a documented phenomenon in numerically unstable deep networks,
modern attention implementations subtract the row-wise maximum from attention logits
before exponentiation specifically to prevent this failure mode, making a direct softmax
overflow an unlikely primary cause on its own. The bounded-versus-unbounded-activation
account developed here is both more precise and better supported by the two-run STEEL
evidence: it explains not only that Informer and Autoformer fail, but specifically why every
other architecture tested — differing from one another substantially in parameter count and
design — does not.

4.6​ Discussion of the Findings

     The multi-phase evaluation conducted in this study provides a nuanced perspective on
the evolution of temporal modeling. The findings suggest that while the transition from
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to Transformer-based architectures offers a significant
leap in predictive precision, this advancement is contingent upon specific data
characteristics and hardware availability.

                                               78
 4.6.1 The Dataset-Conditional Nature of PatchTST's Advantage
      This research affirms that PatchTST is the strongest available architecture for
 multivariate long-term forecasting under practical hardware constraints on datasets with
 rich, higher-dimensional feature structure — decisively so on ETTh1 and Smart Meters. Its
 consistent superiority over the Informer, Autoformer, and RNN baselines is not accidental
 but stems from three synergistic design choices:
●​ Temporal Patching: By segmenting time series into sub-series "patches," the model
   extracts local semantic information while reducing the computational complexity of the
                                     2              2
   attention mechanism from O(𝐿 ) to O (𝑁 ), where the number of patches (𝑁 ) is

   significantly smaller than the sequence length (𝐿).
●​ Channel    Independence:     Unlike   the   Informer,    which    processes all variables
   simultaneously, PatchTST treats each channel as an independent univariate sequence.
   This isolates feature processing and prevents cross-channel interference—a feature that
   proved vital for the heterogeneous variables found in the industrial datasets.
●​ Architectural Efficiency: Despite its high accuracy, PatchTST maintains a relatively
   compact structure compared to the Autoformer. This led to faster training cycles and
   reduced memory usage, making it the most viable high-performance model for
   deployment on standard 6GB VRAM hardware.
      This advantage, however, is not unconditional. On WTH, Informer achieves better
 MAE, MSE, and RMSE than PatchTST, and Informer is the fastest of the three
 Transformers to train on every dataset examined in this chapter. The correct summary is
 therefore not “PatchTST dominates,” but “PatchTST is the strongest choice on rich,
 higher-dimensional data where accuracy is the priority; Informer remains competitive, and
 sometimes superior, on simpler continuous data or where training speed among
 Transformers matters.”


 4.6.2 Lessons from the STEEL Dataset: Bounded Activations versus Unbounded
 Cross-Channel Attention
      The STEEL failure remains the single most important finding of this study, but the
 complete, two-run evidence base developed in Section 4.4.3 supports a materially more
                                               79
precise explanation than was available earlier. The failure is not explained by model
capacity — the smallest model tested succeeds, and a larger model fails — nor by
channel-mixing in general — every recurrent model mixes channels and succeeds. The
evidence instead isolates the specific property shared by the two failing architectures and
absent from all five surviving ones: Informer and Autoformer compute attention or
auto-correlation jointly across channels using unbounded operations, whereas the recurrent
models bound every gate's contribution through sigmoid and tanh nonlinearities, and
PatchTST restricts its attention computation to within-channel patches only.
     This finding carries a stronger and more actionable practical implication than the
earlier, more generic conclusion that “data suitability must precede model selection.” It
suggests a concrete engineering criterion: on industrial data exhibiting extreme scale
heterogeneity, zero-inflation, or discontinuity, architectures that compute unbounded
attention or correlation jointly across raw channels carry a specific, identifiable numerical
risk, independent of their parameter count, and either bounded-activation recurrence or
channel-independent attention are both viable mitigations.


4.6.3 The Accuracy–Efficiency Frontier, Confirmed Across Four Datasets
     The full four-dataset comparison delineates a genuine Pareto frontier rather than a
single dominant model. PatchTST defines the accuracy end of that frontier on feature-rich
datasets while remaining the most parameter-efficient Transformer everywhere; Informer
defines a distinct speed niche among the Transformers, training fastest on every dataset
examined; and the recurrent models, particularly GRU and LSTM-16, define the efficiency
end, training three to twenty-five times faster than any Transformer depending on dataset
scale. The broader implication is that model evaluation must be contextualised jointly by
data characteristics (continuity versus sparsity, feature dimensionality) and deployment
constraints (latency, memory, and accuracy requirements), rather than by accuracy figures
reported in isolation.

                      ​ Conclusion and Future Works

 5.1 Conclusion

     This research investigated the comparative performance of Transformer-based and
recurrent architectures for long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) under realistic
computational constraints, across four datasets of increasing statistical irregularity and, for
two of them, a complete seven-model comparison. The study evaluated Informer,
Autoformer, and PatchTST [7, 8, 9] against LSTM, BiLSTM, GRU, and a deliberately
minimal single-layer LSTM configuration [5, 21, 22], on a single 6GB-VRAM consumer
GPU.
     On WTH, an initial 5-epoch calibration exposed a metric–fidelity dissociation —
Informer's superior summary statistics (MAE 0.51, RMSE 0.69) masked flat,
mean-reverting predictions, while the more volatile Autoformer showed greater latent
trend-following capacity that a longer, 10-epoch budget allowed it to realise. In the
completed three-model comparison, Informer nonetheless emerged as the strongest and
fastest architecture on WTH by MAE, MSE, and RMSE, already complicating any single,
dataset-independent ranking of the three Transformers.
     On ETTh1, PatchTST reversed this ranking, achieving the lowest MAE, MSE, and
RMSE (13.6–25.9% better than Informer) and the only faithful tracking of the cyclical
oil-temperature ground truth, while Informer again produced a mean-collapsed,
near-constant output despite the lowest raw training loss. A subsequent check against the
original Informer publication's own multivariate benchmark found this study's result within
approximately 2% of the published figure, externally validating this thesis's training
pipeline, alongside the disclosed caveat that this study's task configuration and 33-feature
engineered input differ from the original publications' native settings.
     The most consequential finding concerns the STEEL industrial dataset. Across two
independent runs, Informer and Autoformer collapsed to undefined (NaN) losses from
epoch 1 regardless of capacity (large or deliberately reduced to 87K–88K parameters),

                                              81
while every recurrent baseline — including a 4,456-parameter LSTM configuration smaller
than either failing Transformer — and PatchTST trained successfully. Because the smallest
model succeeds while a larger one fails, and every successful recurrent model mixes
channels jointly without failing, neither capacity nor channel-mixing in general explains the
collapse. The evidence instead isolates a specific mechanism: Informer and Autoformer
compute unbounded attention and correlation scores across jointly-mixed channels, whereas
every surviving architecture either bounds its activations through sigmoid/tanh gating or
restricts attention to within-channel patches only [11, 41] — a concrete, actionable criterion
refining this thesis's earlier, less precise account of the same failure.
     The final, largest-scale evaluation — all seven models on the London Smart Meters
dataset — confirmed a genuine accuracy–efficiency Pareto frontier: PatchTST won every
accuracy metric outright (57.6–76.0% better than the six-model average) using the fewest
parameters of any model tested, while the minimal LSTM configuration trained fastest but
was the least accurate model of all seven — in contrast to its competitive performance on
the simpler STEEL dataset, demonstrating that the efficiency floor's accuracy cost is itself
data-dependent.
     This thesis therefore concludes that no single architecture is unconditionally superior
for long-term load forecasting. PatchTST is strongest for accuracy-critical deployment on
rich, higher-dimensional data; Informer offers a genuine speed advantage among
Transformers and can match or exceed PatchTST on simpler data; and recurrent
architectures, particularly GRU, remain preferable wherever training speed or memory
footprint dominate. On industrial data, numerical stability — governed by activation
dynamics rather than parameter count or channel-mixing in general — becomes the
first-order consideration. High-quality, hardware-aware forecasting is achievable under a
6GB VRAM budget, provided model selection is conditioned jointly on data structure and
available compute.

 5.2 Future works

     Several directions follow from this research's findings and limitations:
●​     Confirmatory diagnostics: directly instrument attention-logit magnitudes during
       early STEEL training to empirically confirm the bounded-activation mechanism,
       evaluate   robust,   outlier-resistant    scaling   [59]    as   a   potential   fix   for
       Informer/Autoformer, and complete the outstanding recurrent-baseline comparison
       on WTH and ETTh1.
●​     Efficiency for edge deployment: evaluate structured pruning, quantization [46, 56],
       and knowledge distillation [45], alongside the broader TinyML literature on
       embedded inference [61], to extend this thesis's hardware-aware framing toward
       genuine edge deployment beyond a single consumer GPU.
●​     Interpretability: apply established attention-interpretability methods [58, 64] to
       determine whether learned attention patterns correspond to physically meaningful
       periodicity, and whether early attention instability could be detected before a full
       STEEL-style collapse occurs.
●​     Domain generalisation: extend the accuracy–efficiency and data-suitability
       framework to traffic forecasting [52], financial forecasting [53], and time-series
       anomaly detection [54], each sharing structural properties examined in this thesis.
●​     Hybrid and privacy-aware architectures: evaluate hybrids combining bounded
       recurrent gating with self-attention, such as the Temporal Fusion Transformer [30],
       and federated learning approaches [55] for genuinely individual (rather than
       aggregated) household-level smart-meter data.
●​     Uncertainty and adaptation: extend this thesis's point forecasts to probabilistic
       forecasting [63], and evaluate continual or online learning [62] to handle
       distributional drift in deployed load patterns over time.
     Collectively, these directions extend this thesis's central contribution — a hardware-
and data-aware model-selection framework — toward the broader, ongoing challenge of
reliable load forecasting under real-world deployment constraints [65], as power systems
continue to integrate distributed and renewable generation.
